Module 1 · Lesson 9

Queueing and the cliff

Latency does not rise gently as a system fills up. It stays flat, bends, and then goes vertical, and the bend arrives much earlier than intuition says.

~45 min2 interactives4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 5 and 7
What you can do after this lesson Estimate how latency grows with utilization, explain why variability makes it worse, size a pool or concurrency limit with Little’s law, and say why a consumer that processes slightly less than it receives never catches up.

1. Where waiting comes from

A request that arrives at a busy server waits. The simplest useful model is a single server with random (Poisson) arrivals and random (exponential) service times: the M/M/1 queue. For it, the average time in the system is the service time divided by (1 − ρ), where ρ is utilization, the fraction of time the server is busy.[1] That one formula carries the lesson:

Utilization ρ50%80%90%95%99%
Mean response time (× service time)2×5×10×20×100×

Going from 50% to 90% busy does not make requests 80% slower; it makes them five times slower. And the mean understates it: for M/M/1 first-come-first-served the response time is exponentially distributed, so its p99 is about 4.6 times the mean (ln 100).[1] This is the heart of why “the CPU graph looks fine at 80%” is not reassuring.

2. Watch it happen

The lab simulates 300,000 requests per row with Lindley’s recursion (each request’s wait is the previous wait plus the previous service time minus the gap between arrivals, floored at zero). The formula column lets you check the simulator against theory.

What to notice

  1. The exponential row matches the textbook. Mean response is about 2× at 50%, 5× at 80%, 10× at 90%. The course’s tests check these numerically, so you are not taking the formula on faith.
  2. The tail leaves the mean behind. Compare p99 with the mean as utilization rises; the gap widens. Percentiles (Lesson 7) are where saturation shows up first.
  3. Switch to the high-variance service times. At the same utilization and the same average service time, waiting is several times worse. The general result (the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula for M/G/1 queues) scales waiting with (1 + SCV)/2, where SCV is the squared coefficient of variation of service time. A deterministic server waits half as long as an exponential one; a high-variance one far longer.[1] The formula is standard; the lab’s tests confirm it to within a few percent.
  4. Variance is a design target. Long-tailed service times come from big requests sharing a worker with small ones (head-of-line blocking), lock contention, cache misses, GC pauses. Separating queues by request size, or breaking large work into smaller pieces, lowers the variance term as surely as adding capacity lowers ρ.
  5. Near 99% the simulation itself is noisy. Convergence slows dramatically near saturation. That is the same property that makes real systems at 99% unpredictable.

3. Little’s law: sizing the pool

One more identity does a lot of work: in a stable system, the average number of requests in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends there, L = λW. It needs no assumptions about the arrival or service distributions.[2] That makes it the right tool for connection pools, thread pools, and concurrency limits.

At 500 requests per second and 40 ms each, about 20 are in flight at any moment. Now the dependency slows down fourfold. Your request rate has not changed, but you now need four times the concurrency, so any pool sized for the old number becomes the bottleneck and requests queue behind it. That is how a latency problem in one service turns into pool exhaustion in its callers.

4. Consequences for the rest of the course

  • Headroom is not waste. A service planned to run at 90% pays 10× service time in the average case. Pick a target utilization from your latency SLO (Lesson 10), not from a desire to use the hardware.
  • Retry storms are queueing failures. A retrying client pushes arrival rate toward or past service rate (Lesson 5); once ρ ≥ 1 there is no steady state at all.
  • A backlog drains at the spare capacity. If a Kafka consumer handles μ messages per second and producers send λ < μ, lag drains at μ − λ per second: backlog divided by the spare rate. At λ ≥ μ it never drains. This is the same arithmetic as the 150-request backlog that took five ticks to clear in Lesson 11’s lab.
  • Queues hide in places you may not graph: thread-pool queues, connection-pool waits, accept queues, broker partitions. Lesson 7’s warning about measuring from the right starting point applies to each.

5. Check yourself

Optional: find your real utilization (10 minutes)

Pick one service and find the utilization of its bottleneck resource at peak (thread pool, connection pool, CPU, partition throughput), not the host’s average CPU. Using 1/(1 − ρ), what mean wait does that imply? If it is above 80%, where would the first queue form?
