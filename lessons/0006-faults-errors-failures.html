Module 1 · Lesson 6

Faults, errors, failures, and what redundancy can and cannot do

Three words that engineers use interchangeably have three different meanings, and the difference tells you where to put your defenses.

~40 min2 interactives4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 2 to 4
What you can do after this lesson Describe an incident as a chain of fault, error and failure instead of a single “root cause”, say where in the chain a given defense acts, and explain from a formula why replicas in the same fault domain do not buy the availability they appear to.

1. Three words, three meanings

The standard vocabulary comes from the dependability literature, in a taxonomy paper by Avizienis, Laprie, Randell and Landwehr that is open-access and worth skimming.[1] In it:

  • A fault is the adjudged or hypothesized cause of an error.[2]
  • An error is the part of the system’s state that may lead to a subsequent service failure.[2] Errors can be detected or latent.[3]
  • A failure is the delivered service deviating from correct service: the thing the user sees.[1]

The chain is: a fault creates an error in internal state, and the error may or may not reach the service’s external behavior and become a failure. The point worth tattooing on a runbook: many errors never become failures.[2] Faults can be permanent (a burned-out chip) or transient (a one-off glitch).[2]

Whether a given event is a fault or a failure depends on where you draw the boundary. A payment provider returning errors is a failure of the provider’s service but a fault from your service’s point of view: a possible cause of errors inside your system. (That boundary-relative reading is my explanation of the chain model; check the paper’s discussion of how failures propagate.)

2. Why the distinction pays for itself

“Root cause: the config typo” is the answer to only one question: where did the chain start? It says nothing about why an error was able to reach the customer. Each link has its own defenses, which the taxonomy groups into four means:[1][3] fault prevention, fault tolerance, fault removal, and fault forecasting. Fault tolerance in particular is failure avoidance by error detection and system recovery.[3]

MeansActs onExamples in a MySQL / Kafka / AWS shop (my mapping)
PreventionStops the fault from being introducedCode review, schema constraints, typed config, least-privilege IAM
RemovalFinds and fixes faults before or after releaseTests, canary deploys, post-incident fixes
ToleranceDetects the error and recovers before it becomes a failureRetries with backoff (Lesson 5), replicas, failover, idempotency (Lesson 1)
ForecastingEstimates faults and their consequencesLoad tests, capacity planning, game days

At scale this stops being optional. One practitioner paper puts a number on it: once a service has grown past roughly 10,000 servers and 50,000 disks, failures occur multiple times a day, and a design that needs immediate human action for each one cannot scale.[4] Lesson 11 returns to that paper.

3. What redundancy can and cannot do

The most common tolerance technique is replication. The arithmetic is easy, and it hides a trap. Suppose each replica is independently down a fraction p of the time. Then (assuming instant, perfect failover):

  • one replica: unavailability p
  • two replicas, either may serve: p2
  • three replicas, any one may serve: p3
  • three replicas, a majority must be up (2 of 3): 3p2 − 2p3

With p = 1%, a 2-of-3 majority is unavailable about 0.03% of the time instead of 1%: thirty-three times better. Those formulas assume the replicas fail independently. The trap is that many faults are common-mode: a bad deploy that ships to all replicas, a bug present in every copy, two replicas in the same availability zone or on the same host, a shared dependency like DNS or a certificate. Model that as a probability c that everything fails at once, and every design is stuck at a floor of c, no matter how many replicas you add.

What to notice

  1. At c = 0, going from one replica to three transforms the numbers. Replication works, on paper.
  2. Raise c to even 0.05% and the gap between a single replica and three collapses toward the floor. The best replication cannot beat your largest shared fault.
  3. This is why placement matters more than count. Which of your replicas share a zone, a deploy pipeline, a codebase, a credential, a data store? Each sharing relationship is a path for a common-mode fault. (Which layers of AWS infrastructure share fate is a question for your provider’s documentation; the model only tells you to ask it.)
  4. It also revisits Lesson 3: “replicas fail independently” is itself a model assumption, and one worth writing down.

4. Check yourself

Optional: redraw one incident as a chain (10 minutes)

Take a recent incident writeup and rewrite its timeline as fault, error, failure. Then ask for each link: which defense did we have here, and did it act? You will usually find one link that had none.
