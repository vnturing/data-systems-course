Fan-out on write or on read: describing load

A feed is a materialized view. Where you pay for building it, at write time or read time, is the whole design problem, and the answer is less obvious than the standard story suggests.

~45 min1 interactive4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 5 to 7
What you can do after this lesson Describe a system’s load with the few numbers that drive its design, derive the per-follower cost of pushing versus pulling, and explain why a delivery deadline, not total cost, is usually what forces a hybrid design.

1. Load parameters first

Before choosing an architecture, write down the handful of numbers that characterize the load for your system: requests per second, read-to-write ratio, objects touched per request, and, for anything shaped like a feed, how many recipients one write reaches. Two systems with the same request rate can need opposite designs if one has fan-out of 3 and the other of 3 million.

The classic worked example is a social timeline with two operations: post a tweet and read my home timeline. In 2012, Twitter’s Raffi Krikorian described their architecture in the QCon talk “Timelines at Scale”.[1] A summary of that talk reports roughly 150 million active users and around 300K queries per second for timelines against only ~6,000 requests per second on the write side, and notes that accounts with huge follower lists were becoming a common case rather than an outlier.[2] Treat those as dated, approximate figures: a commentator notes the 300K counts search timelines as well as home timelines,[3] and retellings of the design quote other numbers (write rates anywhere from about 5,000 to 12,000 per second). This lesson therefore builds on the mechanism and uses no Twitter number as an input.

2. Two ways to build a timeline

  • Fan-out on read (pull). Store each tweet once. When a user opens their timeline, fetch recent tweets from everyone they follow and merge. Writes are cheap; every read does work proportional to how many accounts you follow. A multi-way join on every read gets expensive fast.[5]
  • Fan-out on write (push). Keep a precomputed timeline per user. When someone posts, insert the tweet into every follower’s timeline. Reads become a single lookup; the write does work proportional to follower count.[5] Twitter’s version, as summarized, put timelines in a large Redis cluster with each entry replicated across machines.[2]

The cost algebra

Count work per follower per day. Pushing costs tweets per day × cost of one insert. Pulling costs timeline reads per day × cost of one merge. The follower count appears on both sides and cancels. This is my derivation, and it surprises people: in total-work terms, whether a given account is cheaper to push or to pull does not depend on how many followers it has. It depends on the read-to-write ratio and the relative cost of an insert versus a merge. For most feeds reads vastly outnumber writes, which is why precomputing wins for ordinary accounts, but for an author who tweets rarely relative to how often their audience reads, pull can be cheaper in work.

So why does every large feed system end up with a hybrid? Something other than total cost must be binding.

3. The binding constraint is the burst

A single tweet from an account with tens of millions of followers is tens of millions of inserts that must all land quickly. One retelling of the design says a celebrity tweet triggering tens of millions of fan-out writes backs up queues and spikes latency.[5] A case-study write-up of the 2012 talk reports a target that followers see new tweets within about five seconds.[4] (I treat both as secondary and use five seconds only as an illustrative deadline.) That turns the problem into two objectives: total work per day, and the worst-case time to deliver one tweet on the write path, which is the account’s follower count divided by the fan-out throughput.

What to notice

  1. At the defaults, pure fan-out on write has the least total work (tag “least total work”), yet it is infeasible: delivering one tweet from the largest account would take minutes against a five-second deadline. The cheapest feasible design is a hybrid, and the threshold is set by the deadline and the fan-out throughput, not by cost.
  2. Make merges cheap or reads rare, and pure pull becomes both the cheapest and feasible. At the default 60 reads and 3 tweets per user per day the crossover is a merge cost of 0.05× (3 ÷ 60); drag the merge-cost slider below that, or leave it and drop reads to about 10. It is exactly the algebra above: pull wins when reads × merge cost drops below tweets × insert cost.
  3. Raise the insert throughput and the chosen threshold climbs: a faster pipeline can afford to push to bigger accounts. Your delivery pipeline’s capacity is a design input.

4. The hybrid in practice

Retellings describe the hybrid like this: ordinary accounts fan out on write; accounts above a follower threshold are not fanned out, and their recent tweets are merged into each reader’s precomputed timeline at read time.[5] The thresholds quoted vary widely between sources (from about ten thousand to a million followers), which is what you would expect if it is a tuned parameter, not a constant.[5] Two consequences deserve design attention: a user who has been away has an empty or stale precomputed timeline and needs a rebuild on first read, and the read-time merge means celebrity tweets can appear a few seconds later than ordinary ones.[5] Both are the price of bounding the worst case.

Beyond timelines

The same trade appears wherever you maintain a derived view of one write for many readers: notifications, activity feeds, search index updates, denormalized counters, per-tenant caches. In a Kafka pipeline the fan-out workers’ consumer lag is the delivery-time metric (Lesson 10 turns it into an SLI), and Lesson 4’s safety/liveness framing tells you what to do when the pipeline falls behind. Lesson 9 shows why the workers must stay well below saturation.

5. Check yourself

Optional: write the load parameters for one of your systems (10 minutes)

For one feature that fans a write out to many readers, write: writes per second, reads per second, the average and the largest fan-out, and the delivery time you promise. Which of the four sliders in the lab corresponds to a number you could actually measure?
