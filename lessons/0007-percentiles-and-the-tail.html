Module 1 · Lesson 7

Percentiles, the tail, and how to measure latency honestly

The average describes almost no real request. Worse, the usual ways of combining and generating latency numbers can make a bad system look healthy.

~55 min3 interactives4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 5 and 6
What you can do after this lesson Pick the right latency statistic for a decision, compute how fan-out inflates tail latency, explain what hedged requests buy, and spot the two measurement traps (averaged percentiles and coordinated omission) that corrupt dashboards and load tests.

1. The mean lives where no request does

Latency distributions are right-skewed: most requests are fast, a few are very slow. The mean lands between the two groups, so it describes neither. Google’s SRE writing reaches the same conclusion from the other direction: averages can hide outliers,[2] and the summaries of the SRE book’s SLO chapter I read recommend percentile or distribution-based indicators instead.[3] (Read the chapter itself[1] for its exact wording; I verified only its framing of latency as a key indicator.) A percentile is simply a position in the sorted list of response times: p99 is the value that 99% of requests beat.[3]

The lab simulates one minute of traffic on two hosts, one with occasional stalls and one healthy, and shows what happens when you try to summarize the fleet.

The merging trap

A percentile is an order statistic: computing one needs the full sorted distribution, and a finished percentile cannot be recombined. Averaging per-host p99s, or averaging 60 per-minute p99s into an hourly one, yields a number that is not the p99 of anything.[3] Scalable systems keep mergeable summaries instead: histogram buckets, or quantile sketches such as HdrHistogram, t-digest and DDSketch, each with a known error bound.[3] Whatever your metrics stack is, find out whether it stores histograms (mergeable) or precomputed percentiles (not). Look at the lab’s “average of p99s” line against the true fleet p99.

2. The tail at scale

Dean and Barroso’s “The Tail at Scale” (Google, CACM 2013) is the canonical treatment.[4] Their arithmetic: take a server that usually answers in 10 ms but has a 99th-percentile latency of one second. If a request hits one such server, 1 in 100 requests is slow. If a user request must collect responses from 100 such servers in parallel, 63% of user requests will take more than one second. Even at one-in-10,000 per server, a request spanning 2,000 servers sees about one in five slow.[4] A handy recipe for your own system, from Colyer’s summary rather than the paper: take the acceptable percentile (say 0.999) and raise it to the power of the number of servers involved.[5]

Fan-out is not exotic. A page that calls eight microservices, a query that touches every shard, a batch of 50 DynamoDB or MySQL point reads issued in parallel: each is a fan-out, and each inherits the tail.

Tail tolerance

The paper’s response is to build tail-tolerant systems, much as fault-tolerant ones build a reliable whole from unreliable parts.[4] Techniques include:

  • Hedged requests: send a second copy to another server only if the first has been outstanding longer than the 95th-percentile latency. That limits the extra load to about 5% while substantially shortening the tail.[5]
  • Tied requests: enqueue on two servers and cancel the loser. In one real Google system this cut median latency by 16% and the 99.9th percentile by 40%.[5]
  • Micro-partitioning (many more partitions than servers), selective replication of hot items, “good enough” partial results, and canary requests to catch a poisonous request before it hits thousands of servers.[5]

In the lab, hedging after p95 sends about 5% extra calls and rescues the common case enormously (look at the N = 100 median). It does not rescue everything: when both the original and the hedge are slow, the request is still slow, and at large N those double-slow events are common enough to show up at p99. If slowness is correlated (same node, same GC pause, same overloaded dependency), the hedge lands on the same trouble. That is Lesson 6’s common-mode fault in latency clothing. Note also the cost side of hedging: extra calls are retries, so everything in Lesson 5 (idempotency, budgets) applies.

3. Coordinated omission

The last trap is in how latency is generated and recorded. Gil Tene named it coordinated omission. A load generator that waits for each reply before sending the next request silently stops sending during a stall, so it omits exactly the samples that would have been worst. In his worked example, each ~536 ms freeze at a 1 ms target interval leaves roughly 535 missing observations; he adds that omission is harmless only when it is random, and that this is a measurement-methodology problem in load testers, not a problem in the system under test.[6] Load tools differ: some default to a closed workload model, others offer arrival-rate (open) models that send on schedule regardless of replies.[7]

The server and the freeze are identical in both rows. The closed-loop tool reports a p99 of well under a millisecond, because it records one slow request instead of the hundreds that real users would have issued during the freeze. An open workload (clients that do not slow down because your service is slow) is the realistic model for public APIs; a closed one is right only for genuinely closed systems such as a fixed worker pool draining a queue.[7] Look for the same problem in production measurements taken only at the server: if your metrics start counting at request receipt, time spent waiting in an accept queue never appears.

4. A practical rulebook (my synthesis)

  1. Report p50, p99 and p99.9 side by side, never the mean alone. Keep the mean only as a resource metric (total time spent serving) that sums correctly.
  2. Store histograms or sketches; never average percentiles.
  3. For a fan-out of N, compute the effective percentile as qN. Set per-call targets from the request-level target, not the other way around.
  4. Load-test with an open, constant-arrival-rate model, and compare against a scheduled send time.
  5. Be suspicious of low-traffic p99s: with few requests one slow call moves the number.

5. Check yourself

Optional: audit one dashboard (15 minutes)

Find a latency graph you rely on. Is it a mean, a percentile, or a histogram? If it shows a percentile aggregated across instances or time, can the system merge them correctly? Where does the timer start: at the client, the load balancer, or the application? Write down what could be hiding before the timer starts.
