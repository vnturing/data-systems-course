Module 0 · Lesson 3

System models: say your assumptions out loud

Every claim like “this is safe” or “this fails over correctly” is secretly conditional. A system model is how you write the condition down.

~40 min1 interactive4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 1 and 2
What you can do after this lesson Write a three-line system model for any design, spot the claims in a design doc that silently depend on an assumption you do not hold, and explain why every timeout-based failover in your stack is a trade-off and not a fact.

1. The sentence that makes models necessary

In the previous two lessons you reasoned about what a client could and couldn't know, and what a crash could leave behind. Each argument used assumptions: that a crashed process loses memory but not its disk; that a reply might be delayed but not forged. A system model is the explicit list of such assumptions. One university course built on Kleppmann's lecture notes states the stakes in one line: if your fault assumptions are wrong, all bets are off.[1]

This matters in practice because a correctness argument is only valid inside its model. “Our lock is safe” is a claim about a model. When someone asks “but what if the lock holder pauses for 30 seconds?”, they are not nitpicking; they are asking whether your model includes pauses.

2. Three axes, pick one value on each

The standard framing has you choose a behavior for the nodes, the network, and time:[1]

AxisOptions (weakest assumption last)What each says
Nodescrash-stop → crash-recovery → Byzantine Crash-stop: a node may crash at any time and then never executes again. Crash-recovery: it may crash and later resume, having lost in-memory state (durable state survives). Byzantine: it may behave arbitrarily, including maliciously.
Networkreliable → fair-loss (best-effort) → arbitrary Fair-loss: a message sent repeatedly is eventually delivered, but any single message may be lost, duplicated or reordered.[2] Arbitrary: an adversary can interfere with messages.
Timingsynchronous → partially synchronous → asynchronous Synchronous: delays have a known upper bound. Asynchronous: no timing assumptions at all; no timeouts, no clocks. Partially synchronous: behaves synchronously most of the time but sometimes exceeds its bounds for network delay, process pauses, and clock drift.[2][3]

Real systems usually land on crash-recovery nodes, fair-loss links, partial synchrony, with Byzantine behavior assumed away (checksums and TLS handle the cheap parts of it). The Lesson 1 simulator lives in exactly that world, so you have already been reasoning in this model.

Mapping your stack (this table is my judgment, not from a source)

Orchestrator kills and restarts a service; its MySQL data is on a durable volumeCrash-recovery node. Memory is lost, committed data survives.

3. Why time is the axis that bites: failure detection

The key practical question in this lesson is: how do you know a node is dead? You send it heartbeats and declare it dead if you stop hearing them. That is a timeout, and Lesson 1 already told you what a timeout means: only that nothing arrived in time.

The model-theoretic answer is sharp. A perfect timeout-based failure detector (never wrong) is possible only in a synchronous system with crash-stop nodes and reliable links. Real systems are partially synchronous or asynchronous. There, an eventually perfect detector is possible, which may temporarily mislabel a healthy node as crashed.[1] Notice the word “temporarily”: false suspicion is not a defect of a particular implementation; it is the price of the model.

Now feel it. Choose a timeout. The lab replays the same simulated day of heartbeats against each setting.

What to notice

  1. Two costs pull in opposite directions. Shorter timeouts notice real crashes sooner and also suspect healthy nodes more often. There is no free setting, only a cheapest one for the costs you put in.
  2. In bounded mode the false suspicions hit zero. That happens only because the simulated stalls have a known worst case, which makes the world effectively synchronous. Knowing a bound is exactly the extra assumption “synchronous” buys you.
  3. Turn on heavy-tailed stalls. Now no timeout is both fast and clean; false suspicions fall slowly but do not vanish. That is the partially-synchronous world: the bound exists eventually or sometimes but you do not know it.
  4. The simulator's numbers are made up. Its job is to show the shape of the trade-off. Your real stall distribution is something you can measure (look at the longest gaps between heartbeats in your own metrics) and that measurement is far more valuable than any default.

4. Where this shows up in your day job

Every automated failover has a timeout buried in it: a load balancer's health-check threshold, a replica-promotion tool's “primary unreachable for N seconds”, a service-discovery TTL. Each is a heartbeat detector. When you tune one, you are moving along the curve in the lab: faster recovery from real failures against more spurious failovers, which have their own cost (a needless failover can itself cause the outage). Lesson 4 gives the vocabulary for that trade; Module 8 goes deep on how pauses defeat leases and what fencing tokens do about it.

A worked example of a hidden assumption “The leader holds a 10-second lease and renews every 3 seconds, so there is never more than one leader.” Which model is that claim in? One where a process cannot be paused for longer than the lease without noticing. Under partial synchrony a 25-second pause eventually happens: the old leader wakes up still believing it holds the lease while a new leader exists. The claim was true in a synchronous model and false in yours.

Write your model in one line at the top of every design doc: Nodes: crash-recovery. Network: fair-loss, partitions possible. Timing: partially synchronous (pauses up to ~X s observed). Then check each safety claim against it.

5. Check yourself

Optional: write your own model line (10 minutes)

For one system you run, write the three-line model (nodes, network, timing) and underline any claim in its design doc that needs a stronger assumption than you wrote. If you have monitoring on heartbeat or health-check gaps, note the longest gap you can find in a month; that is your first real measurement of the timing axis.
