Module 1 · Lesson 10

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

“Reliable” is not a number. This lesson turns it into one, and turns the number into a way to decide when to ship and when to stop.

~45 min2 interactives4-question checkBuilds on Lessons 6 to 9
What you can do after this lesson Write an SLI and SLO for a service, convert an SLO into a concrete error budget in minutes or requests, read an incident as budget burned and burn rate, and see why a service’s availability is capped by the dependencies it cannot do without.

1. Three terms, three audiences

The vocabulary comes from Google’s SRE book. An SLI (service level indicator) is a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service provided. An SLO (objective) is a target value or range of values for a service level measured by an SLI; a natural form is “SLI ≤ target” or “lower bound ≤ SLI ≤ upper bound”. An SLA (agreement) is an explicit or implicit contract with users that includes consequences of meeting or missing the SLOs it contains.[1] So: the SLI is the measurement, the SLO is the internal target, the SLA is the contract with consequences. SLAs usually sit looser than the SLOs behind them.[3]

The chapter also makes two points that save a lot of arguments. You should not use every metric you can track as an SLI; understanding what users want should drive the selection of a few indicators.[1] And even a service with no SLA benefits from defining SLIs and SLOs to manage the service.[1]

2. Choosing indicators that mean something

  • Start from user value, not from what you can measure. Google’s Cloud Reliability Engineers describe picking SLOs that represent the most critical aspects of the user experience.[2]
  • Prefer threshold counts over averages for latency. “99% of requests complete in under 300 ms” is an SLI-plus-target built from a fraction, and it is the same statement as “p99 ≤ 300 ms”. Using two thresholds is common: their example SLO table pairs a tight bound that most requests should meet with a loose bound that nearly all must (50% under 250 ms and 99% under 3,000 ms).[2] This is Lesson 7 in SLO form: a mean cannot be a promise to any individual user.
  • Count slowness as failure when it is. The same source treats requests that take longer than 30 seconds as if the service were down, counting them against availability.[2]
  • Measure as near the user as you can. Client-side latency is often more relevant, but if you can only measure at the server, that is a proxy and the SLI should say so.[1] Lesson 7’s timer-starts-too-late problem applies directly.

3. The error budget

If the SLO is 99.9%, the system is allowed to be bad 0.1% of the time. That allowance is the error budget: 1 − SLO.[3] Its value is organizational: while budget remains, releases and experiments may proceed; when it is spent, reliability work takes priority. The SRE book frames it as: as long as there is error budget remaining, new releases can be pushed.[4] Each additional nine cuts the budget tenfold while the cost of delivering it climbs steeply; the book warns that an incremental improvement may cost 100 times more than the previous one.[4]

Burn rate

One number makes incidents comparable: burn rate = observed error rate ÷ allowed error rate (my definition; the formula is simple). Burn rate 1 spends the budget exactly over the window. A total outage against a 99.9% SLO burns at 1,000× and would consume a 30-day budget in about 43 minutes; 5% errors burn at 50×, lasting about 14 hours. Burn rate is what tells you how urgent an incident is, which a raw error count cannot.

What to notice

  1. A 99.9% SLO leaves about 43 minutes a month. Two ordinary incidents can spend it. Slide to 99.99% and the budget is under five minutes; at 99.999% it is about 26 seconds. A single TCP-level hiccup can exceed that.
  2. The same incident list is fine at 99.5% and a freeze at 99.9%. The SLO, not the incident, decides what the incidents mean. Choosing the SLO is choosing a policy.
  3. Small, long incidents can cost as much as short, total ones. Compare the 90-minute 5%-error incident to a 4.5-minute total outage: identical budget cost, wildly different burn rate and urgency.
  4. The budget model assumes uniform traffic. If your traffic is bursty, an outage at peak costs more users than the same minutes at night. Count requests, not minutes, if the difference matters.

4. Dependencies set a ceiling

If your request needs several services to all work, and they fail independently, the availabilities multiply. Five hard dependencies at 99.9% give about 99.5% before your own bugs add anything.

Two consequences. First, a 99.95% SLO for a service with five 99.9% hard dependencies is a promise that requires either luck or a change in structure: make some dependencies optional (degrade instead of fail), or make them redundant with a design that avoids Lesson 6’s common-mode trap. Second, the multiplication assumes independence, which is exactly the assumption Lesson 6 taught you to doubt: two dependencies that share a zone or a provider fail together, so the real availability is usually better than the product when failures are positively correlated, and your incidents are lumpier.

Finally, the SRE book’s counterintuitive advice: don’t over-achieve.[5] A service consistently far better than its SLO sets user expectations the SLO does not promise, and dependents start relying on it. (Summarized from the book; I did not read this passage directly.)

5. Check yourself

Optional: draft one SLO (15 minutes)

For one user-facing service, write: the user journey you care about, one availability SLI (good requests over valid requests), one latency SLI as a threshold count, targets for each, the 30-day budget each implies, and what you would do the day it is spent. If you cannot name the action, the SLO is decorative.
