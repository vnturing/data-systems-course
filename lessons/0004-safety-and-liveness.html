Safety and liveness: what “correct” means

“Never lose data” and “stay available” are different kinds of promise. Kafka’s durability settings are the clearest place to see the difference.

~45 min2 interactives4-question checkBuilds on Lesson 3
What you can do after this lesson State any requirement as a safety or a liveness property, explain why the two behave differently under failure, and set acks, min.insync.replicas and unclean.leader.election.enable for a topic by choosing, on purpose, what you are willing to give up.

1. Two kinds of promise

Informally, going back to Lamport: a safety property says something bad never happens; a liveness property says something good eventually happens.[2] The formal version, from Alpern and Schneider’s 1985 paper “Defining Liveness”, makes the difference testable.[1][2]

The finite-prefix test Safety: if an execution violates the property, you can point to a finite prefix of it, a moment in the log, where the damage is already done and no continuation can repair it.
Liveness: no finite prefix ever refutes it. However badly things look so far, some continuation could still satisfy it ("while there's life there's hope").[1]

Practical version: can I put my finger on the moment it went wrong? If yes, you were looking at a safety violation. If the honest answer is only “it hasn’t happened yet”, it is liveness. A classic result (the decomposition theorem) says any property can be written as a combination of a safety property and a liveness property, which is why specifications are usually stated as one of each.[1][2]

RequirementKindWhy
No acknowledged Kafka record is ever lostsafetyThe loss is a specific moment: an acked id absent from the surviving log
A customer is never charged twice for one ordersafetyThe second charge is the observable moment
Two nodes are never both primary for the same shardsafetyTwo simultaneous primaries is a snapshot you can photograph
Every produce request eventually succeedslivenessA stall of any finite length is not yet a violation
A failed primary is eventually replacedliveness“Eventually” has no deadline, so no finite prefix refutes it
Every payment eventually settleslivenessSame shape: good thing, no deadline

A caveat that surprises people: add a deadline (“replaced within 30 seconds”) and the requirement becomes a safety property in this formal sense, because missing the deadline is a moment you can point at. That is why SLOs behave like safety properties you can alert on, while “eventually” promises are the ones you cannot. (This bounded-liveness observation is standard in the specification literature but is not drawn from the sources below; treat it as my framing.)

2. Why the split drives design

Lesson 3 left you in a partially synchronous world where messages can be lost, nodes can pause, and you cannot tell slow from dead. In that world you can often keep safety unconditionally while liveness is conditional on the system behaving for long enough. This is my synthesis, not a quote, but it is the organizing idea behind most consensus protocols you will meet in Module 9: stalls and false suspicions (Lesson 3) may cost you progress; they must never cost you correctness. When someone proposes a design in which a pause or a spurious failover can corrupt data, they have let a liveness problem leak into safety.

3. Kafka as the working example

A Kafka partition has one leader and some followers. The in-sync replicas (ISR) are the set of replicas currently caught up with the leader. Three settings decide what “acknowledged” means and what happens when replicas fail:

SettingWhat it controlsMoves you toward
acks (producer)acks=all: the producer sees success only after the record is replicated to all in-sync replicas.[3] acks=1 or 0: the producer continues even if replicas are unavailable.[3]safety (all) / liveness (1, 0)
min.insync.replicasWith acks=all, the minimum number of replicas that must acknowledge. If it cannot be met the producer gets an exception (NotEnoughReplicas or NotEnoughReplicasAfterAppend). A typical setup is replication factor 3, min ISR 2, acks=all.[4]safety, at the cost of rejecting writes
unclean.leader.election.enableIf true, an out-of-sync replica can become leader when no in-sync replica is alive: availability is preserved but data may be lost. If false and no live in-sync replica exists, the partition is unavailable.[5]liveness (true) / safety (false)
Check your defaults Older documentation states that unclean election defaults to true (a Cloudera page for an older Kafka does exactly that).[5] Current references list the default as false.[6] Do not trust either from memory: read the effective value on your own cluster and topic, since managed services and old clusters may differ.

4. Break a partition on purpose

This is a deliberately simplified model (it notices failures instantly, ignores batching and leader epochs). It is built to expose the trade-off, not to certify Kafka behavior. Try these experiments in order:

  1. Set acks=1 and click Run preset: Leader dies right after an acks=1 write. Note which acknowledged message is lost, and that broker A still has it on disk. It is lost the moment a new leader without it accepts writes.
  2. Switch to acks=all, min ISR 2 and run the same preset. Nothing is lost. What did you pay? Look for the first rejected produce in the next experiment.
  3. Run the cascading-failure preset with unclean = false. Watch the partition go offline and stay offline until the right broker returns. Then flip unclean = true and run it again: the partition comes back sooner, and a message you were told was safe is gone.
  4. Bonus: with acks=all and min ISR 1, produce, kill C, produce, kill B, produce (accepted by the leader alone), then Destroy A disk. Look at the “on no disk anywhere” tag. Repeat with min ISR 2.

Eight configurations, three disasters

Reading the matrix:

  • min.insync.replicas does nothing when acks=1. The rows for acks=1 are identical across min ISR. (This is by design: the setting applies when the producer sets acks=all.[4])
  • acks=all alone is not durability. With min ISR 1, once the ISR has shrunk to the leader, acks=all acknowledges from the leader alone. It behaves like acks=1 in the cascade.
  • Unclean election is the lever for loss in the cascade. Every unclean=true row loses acknowledged messages, even acks=all with min ISR 2.
  • Min ISR 2 is what survives a lost disk. In the third scenario acks=all with min ISR 1 still destroys an acknowledged record, because once the ISR shrank to the leader, “all in-sync replicas” meant one copy. With min ISR 2 that write is rejected instead of acknowledged, so nothing acknowledged lives on a single disk.
  • The safe row costs liveness. Only acks=all, min ISR 2, unclean=false loses nothing in all three scenarios, and it rejects the most produce requests (3 of 5 in the cascade). That is the trade, quantified: safety is unconditional, so the price is paid in availability.

5. The producer side is half the story

The documentation lists two exceptions for an unmet minimum: NotEnoughReplicas and NotEnoughReplicasAfterAppend.[4] As its name suggests, the second can be reported after the leader has already appended the record (confirm the exact semantics in the Kafka docs for your version). If so, the producer is told the write failed while it may in fact exist, and a producer that retries then risks writing the record twice. That is Lesson 1 again, and the reason producers should enable idempotence: since Kafka 0.11.0.0 the idempotent producer prevents duplicates caused by the producer’s own retries.[7] A safe pipeline needs both: broker settings that protect acknowledged data, and a producer that does not double-write when it retries.

6. Deciding per topic (my judgment)

Topic carriesLean towardTypical settings
Billing, ledger, order eventsSafety. A short unavailability is cheaper than a lost record.acks=all, replication factor 3, min ISR 2, unclean off, idempotent producer
Metrics, logs, clickstream where loss is tolerableLiveness. Prefer to keep accepting writes.Possibly acks=1 and, deliberately, unclean on. One source notes enabling it for use cases like metrics and log collection, and calls it dangerous without understanding the consequences.[6]

Whichever you choose, write the choice down as a pair: “This topic never loses an acknowledged record (safety); it may reject produces for up to the time it takes to restore two in-sync replicas (liveness we sacrificed).” A requirement you cannot phrase as a safety-versus-liveness pair is usually a requirement you have not finished thinking about.

7. Check yourself

Optional: look at one real topic (10 minutes)

Find the effective min.insync.replicas, unclean.leader.election.enable and replication factor for one topic you care about, and the producer’s acks and idempotence setting. Write the safety/liveness pair those settings actually implement. If it differs from what the team believes, that difference is worth knowing.
