Compaction strategies and the three amplifications

Compaction is where an LSM-tree pays for its fast writes. The strategy you choose decides who pays: the disk bandwidth, the disk capacity, or the reader.

~50 min1 interactivecalibrated on real RocksDB4-question check
What you can do after this lesson Define write, read and space amplification, predict which compaction strategy suits a workload, explain what bloom filters and caches change, and say why disk headroom matters for tiered compaction.

1. Three numbers

  • Write amplification: bytes physically written ÷ bytes the application wrote.
  • Read amplification: disk reads needed per lookup.
  • Space amplification: bytes on disk ÷ bytes of live data.

They trade against each other. The RUM conjecture states this: Athanassoulis and colleagues define read, update and memory overheads for an access method and conjecture that optimizing along two of them negatively impacts the third.[1] It is a conjecture, not a theorem, which is a useful reminder to measure instead of assuming. The three strategies below are three different places to stand on that triangle.

2. Two ways to compact

Leveled. Levels have exponentially growing target sizes (RocksDB’s documentation describes a size multiplier between levels, 10 by default[2]); within a level, files do not overlap. A lookup consults at most one file per level plus the overlapping files in level 0. Compaction picks a file and merges it with the overlapping files one level down, rewriting data repeatedly as it descends, which is write amplification.

Size-tiered. Runs accumulate; when several of similar size exist they are merged into one bigger run. Data is rewritten less often (roughly once per tier), but many overlapping runs coexist, so reads consult more of them and dead versions linger longer.

My mini-LSM implements classic size-tiered compaction (as used by Cassandra), which is not the same algorithm as RocksDB’s “universal” style: universal also triggers large merges to bound space amplification and keeps runs in age order so a read can stop at the first match. I show real universal numbers next to the model’s size-tiered ones, and they agree on direction but not on every magnitude.

3. Measure it

The lab runs three workloads against four policies. Choose a workload, toggle bloom filters, and set a cache.

Here is what real RocksDB did on the same shapes, with the range across repeated runs (compression off, 108-byte records, 4 MB memtable):

What to notice

  1. Tiered writes less and stores more. On the update-heavy workload the model’s write amplification is about 2 to 3 for tiered against 4.6 to 4.8 for leveled, matching the real direction. The price is space: peak space amplification of 3.1 to 3.9× for tiered against about 1.3 for leveled. Real universal ended at 1.6 to 1.8. You provision disks for the peak, which is why the lab reports it.
  2. Bloom filters collapse absent-key reads. Untick and tick the filter box: absent-key reads fall by more than 20× for every policy. The filter has no effect on a present key, which still needs the block that holds it (about 0.9 disk reads when most of the data is not cached). The filter’s false-positive rate in the model measured 0.96% against a theoretical 0.8% for 10 bits per key.
  3. More runs, more reads. The “merge at 6 runs” policy ends with 10 sorted runs and needs about 8 reads per lookup without filters. Real universal sat at 4 runs and 3.3 reads. Fewer, larger runs help readers.
  4. The cache hides the small runs. The model assumes the smallest runs live in cache first, as the real measurement’s cache (8 MB) covered the two smallest levels. A bigger cache lowers reads without touching write cost.
  5. Keys in order change everything. Choose the third workload. Leveled compaction approaches 1× because nothing overlaps (Lesson 19). The model’s tiered policy still merges disjoint runs and stays near 3×; I did not measure real universal on sequential keys, so treat that row as a property of the textbook policy, not of RocksDB.
Do not generalize one number RocksDB’s own “dynamic level bytes” option (levels sized from the bottom up to bound space amplification[2]) changed space amplification only from 1.19 to 1.14 in this test. Across repeated runs the leveled absent-key read cost ranged from 1.3 to 2.1 because the final layout had 4 or 5 sorted runs. Every figure here depends on data size, key pattern, cache, and run-to-run timing.

5. Check yourself

Optional: check your headroom (15 minutes)

For an LSM-based store you run, find its compaction style, the free disk it needs to compact (a full-size merge temporarily needs room for old and new files), and its disk-usage graph. Does the graph show sawtooth swings? The height of the teeth is your space-amplification peak.
